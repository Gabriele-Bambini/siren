## 1. Structure prediction: antibodies, nanobodies, CDR-H3 accuracy, Ab-Ag docking, failure modes

### Takeaway
Antibody (Fv) and nanobody monomer structure prediction is largely solved except for CDR-H3, where specialised models still sit at roughly 2.4-2.9 Å mean RMSD and gains between generations are small (tenths of an Å). Antibody-antigen docking is not solved: AlphaFold3 (AF3) is best among the open and closed co-folding models but fails on about 50-65% of antibody/nanobody-antigen targets with a single seed, and "high accuracy" (DockQ >= 0.8) docking happens in only about 10-13% of cases. Boltz-1, Chai-1 and Boltz-2 trail AF3. Success tracks getting the H3 loop right and is helped by massive sampling, ipTM-based ranking and physics- or epitope-restrained refinement.

### Cited Findings

**Fv / nanobody monomer predictors (CDR-H3 RMSD)**
- ABodyBuilder2 (ImmuneBuilder): CDR-H3 RMSD 2.81 Å on a benchmark of 34 recently solved antibodies, only 0.09 Å better than AlphaFold-Multimer and >100x faster — [search summary of the ImmuneBuilder paper](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10227038/); [Commun Biol version](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/s42003-023-04927-7). A later Zenodo record describes a benchmark of 40 antibodies, so benchmark size differs between versions — [Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/record/7258553).
- NanoBodyBuilder2: CDR-H3 average RMSD 2.89 Å, 0.55 Å better than AlphaFold2 — [ImmuneBuilder](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10227038/).
- In the ImmuneBuilder paper, IgFold and EquiFold produce CDR-H3 loops about 10% less accurate than ABodyBuilder2 and AlphaFold-Multimer (antibodies, not nanobodies) — [ImmuneBuilder](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10227038/).
- ABodyBuilder3 (Kenlay et al., Bioinformatics 2024;40(10):btae576): preprint Table 1 mean CDR-H3 RMSD of 2.54 Å (ABodyBuilder2), 2.42 Å (ABodyBuilder3) and 2.40 Å (ABodyBuilder3-LM, which uses language-model embeddings); OpenMM and YASARA refinement baselines 2.53 and 2.49 Å. A pLDDT-style head predicts per-residue error; CDR-H3 Pearson correlation of predicted uncertainty with RMSD is 0.61 (ABB3), 0.73 (ABB3-LM) and 0.57 (ABB2). The arXiv, Bioinformatics and ICML-workshop versions describe the base model differently, so check the published PDF — [arXiv preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.20863v1); [search listing incl. Bioinformatics citation](https://mlanthology.org/icmlw/2024/kenlay2024icmlw-abodybuilder3). Note the ABB2 figure (2.54 Å) differs from the 2.81 Å above because the benchmark set differs; cross-paper numbers are not comparable.
- IgFold (Ruffolo, Gray et al., Nat Commun 2023;14:2389): language model pre-trained on 558M natural antibody sequences plus graph networks that predict backbone coordinates directly; reported to match or beat alternatives including AlphaFold in under 25 s per prediction. The paper notes AlphaFold-Multimer performance on antibodies had not been extensively validated — [IgFold paper (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10129313); [search listing](https://doaj.org/article/2e4b6273286146868eb6be0826773b5b). I did not retrieve IgFold's own CDR-H3 table.
- tFold-Ab: a survey table lists 2.74 Å on a SAbDab-based benchmark and another table gives 3.03 Å on SAbDab-22H1; the survey warns IgFold and tFold-Ab use slightly different benchmarking, so numbers are not directly comparable — [survey (arXiv 2402.08703)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.08703); [search listing](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10361724/table/T1).
- H3-OPT reports 2.24 Å average RMSD (Cα) on CDR-H3 loops and compares against AlphaFold2 and IgFold — [H3-OPT (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11208048/).
- 2023 six-tool nanobody comparison (incl. IgFold, NanoNet, AlphaFold2, OmegaFold, ESMFold): tailoring an AI method to antibodies does not necessarily improve nanobody results, and CDR3 modelling remains a big challenge for all tools — [search listing of the study](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202304.0195). Per-tool numbers not retrieved.
- Ibex (Prescient Design, arXiv 2507.09054; ICML 2025): pan-immunoglobulin (antibody, nanobody, TCR) model trained on labelled apo/holo pairs so it can predict bound or unbound conformation. Evaluated on a private dataset; no independent benchmark found — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09054); [ICML page](https://icml.cc/virtual/2025/51303); [README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/prescient-design/ibex/2e785563806a3b63600eeaa2107d1254ddf5d196/README.md).

**Antibody/nanobody-antigen complexes**
- FoldBench (Nat Commun 2025), 172 antibody-antigen pairs, success = DockQ >= 0.23: AF3 is the only model above 40% (45.4%); the same search summary also quotes 47.9% for AF3, and other methods fail on >60% (the 45.4% vs 47.9% discrepancy is not resolved in my sources). Nanobodies: AF3 53.3% overall success with 33.3% high quality. Boltz-1 scored 0.8% high-quality docking. The paper concludes Ab-Ag prediction remains particularly challenging with failure rates >50% — [FoldBench](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-67127-3).
- Gray-lab study "What does AlphaFold3 learn about antibody and nanobody docking, and what remains unsolved?" (mAbs 2025; bioRxiv 2024): single-seed AF3 high-accuracy docking success 10.2% (antibodies) and 13.3% (nanobodies); Boltz-1 4.08% / 5%, Chai-1 0% / 3.33%; overall antibody success (DockQ > 0.23) 34.7%. Boltz-2 was excluded because its training cutoff (1 Jun 2023) is later than AF3's (30 Sep 2021) — [mAbs paper (search listing)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/19420862.2025.2545601); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12360200/).
- Same study, other quantities (figures vary by version: single-seed failure is reported as 65% in one version and 60% in another): 20 seeds suffice for stable median CDR-H3 RMSD; the main analysis used 3 seeds x 5 diffusion decoys. A correct H3 loop is nearly necessary for high-accuracy docking (about 96.9% of high-accuracy models have a correct H3) but not sufficient (a correct H3 yields high-accuracy docking only about 55.8% of the time for antibodies and 36.2% for nanobodies). ipTM correlates with true DockQ for top-ranked complexes (R = 0.90 antibodies, R = 0.73 nanobodies); proposed cutoffs are ipTM-HA >= 0.5 (antibodies) and >= 0.4 (nanobodies), and adding binding-energy terms improves discrimination — [bioRxiv v2 (search listing)](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.09.21.614257v2.full); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12360200/).
- Boltz-2 vs AF3: the Boltz-2 preprint reports improvement over Boltz-1 but still lags AF3, most visibly on unseen antigens — [Boltz-2 preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.06.14.659707v1.full.pdf). A 2026 Frontiers in Bioinformatics paper reports Boltz-2 confidence measures most consistently rank true nanobody binders highly, while the best single ranking measures come from other models — [Frontiers 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioinformatics/articles/10.3389/fbinf.2026.1858891/full). I found no dedicated Boltz-2 antibody docking success-rate number.
- Chai-1 with epitope restraints (secondary source, benchmark not stated): mean DockQ 43.7 (Chai-1r with 4 epitope residues), 35.6 (Chai-1), 26.4 (Boltz), 20.6 (AlphaFold2) — [Tamarind blog](https://www.tamarind.bio/blog/how-well-does-alphafold3-work-antibodies-and-nanobodies).
- OpenDDE (open-source engine, arXiv 2607.03787, July 2026 preprint, not verified): reports 51.0% on PXMeter-AB, 70.0% on FoldBench-AB and 66.4% on a "2026ARK-AB" benchmark. Success definition and baselines not seen; the 70.0% FoldBench-AB value is far above the ~45-48% AF3 value in FoldBench itself, so definitions or subsets likely differ — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.03787).
- CASP16 (Proteins 2025): complex prediction is not solved; >30% of targets, particularly antibody-antigen, were highly challenging and each group got only about a quarter of them right. The kozakovvajda group significantly outperformed others on antibody targets (abstract truncated in my source). Most groups used AlphaFold-Multimer or AF3; top groups gained from optimised MSAs, partial-sequence constructs and massive sampling plus selection. Organisers say more antibody targets are needed to judge progress — [CASP16 complex assessment](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12750043/); [CASP16 oligomer slides](https://predictioncenter.org/CASP16/doc/presentations/Day-2/Day2-04-Cong-oligomers_redacted.pdf).
- AlphaRED (AlphaFold + Rosetta ReplicaDock, eLife): published version 63% acceptable-or-better on Docking Benchmark 5.5 and 43% on antibody-antigen targets, where AlphaFold-Multimer gets 20% (the preprint claimed 66% and 51% vs 19%; use the published numbers). Physics docking takes 6-8 h on a 24-core CPU cluster, and success was only 33% on flexible targets (RMSD_UB >= 2.2 Å) — [eLife](https://elifesciences.org/articles/94029); [eLife reviewed preprint](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/94029v1).
- Informal check: a student-journal study of AlphaFold on 40 nanobody-antigen complexes found 35 of 40 in the DockQ "incorrect" class. Low-authority source, model version unclear — [Emerging Investigators](https://emerginginvestigators.org/articles/24-111).

**Failure modes: flexibility, ensembles, antigen-specific loops**
- AF2 predicts antibody frameworks accurately but misplaces H3; weak evolutionary signal in CDRs and low output diversity limit it. A 2025 pipeline (AlphaFlow ensembles + clustering + HADDOCK, Xu/Giulini/Bonvin) found broader H3 sampling improved docking success — [bioRxiv / PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12279294/).
- ITsFlexible (Nat Mach Intell 2025): GNN trained on about 1.2M PDB loop structures (>100,000 unique sequences) classifies CDR3 loops as rigid or flexible, generalises to MD simulations, and was checked by cryo-EM on three CDR-H3 loops. Flexibility is framed as classification, not single-structure prediction — [search listing](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12279294/) (the listing groups it with the 2025 ensemble literature; check the original NMI paper).
- Earlier metadynamics/classical-MD work treats antibodies as conformational ensembles and uses metadynamics to overcome the sampling limits imposed by high energy barriers; the search summary did not name the paper, and the closest hit was a CDR-H3 loop-ensemble study — [PMC6330313](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6330313/) (attribution of the metadynamics claim to this exact paper is unverified).
- Related design-side evidence on what is predictable: Ibex's apo/holo training explicitly targets bound vs unbound loop states, which is the "antigen-specific conformation" problem — [Ibex arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09054).

### Inferences
- The last three generations of Fv predictors (ABB2 to ABB3, IgFold, tFold-Ab) differ by tenths of an Å on CDR-H3 against a roughly 2-3 Å floor; the remaining error is probably dominated by conformational heterogeneity and antigen-induced fit, not model capacity. Single-structure RMSD against crystal structures is a ceiling-limited metric.
- For design, the useful, reliable outputs are (a) Fv structure for developability features (patch-based descriptors, TAP-style), where H3 error matters less, and (b) a calibrated confidence (ABB3 pLDDT, AF3 ipTM) used as a filter. Complex prediction is currently good for triage (ipTM thresholds) but not as ground truth for affinity.
- The CDR-H3 correctness to docking relationship (necessary, not sufficient) suggests the two problems (loop conformation, interface placement) should be evaluated separately in any new benchmark.
- Because Ab-Ag docking benchmarks differ in seeds, cutoff dates, antibody-vs-nanobody mix and success thresholds, 35% (single-seed AF3, DockQ >= 0.23), 45-48% (FoldBench) and 66-70% (OpenDDE preprint) should not be read as a trend.

### Gaps
- Primary papers not opened (blocked): AF3 (Abramson et al., Nature 2024), Boltz-1, Boltz-2, Chai-1, Protenix, tFold-Ab, DeepAb, NanoNet. I have no sourced AF3-paper antibody-antigen figure and no Protenix antibody number.
- IgFold's own CDR-H3 RMSD table and nanobody numbers; per-tool CDR3 numbers from the 2023 nanobody comparison; NanoBodyBuilder2 vs NanoNet numbers.
- A single like-for-like 2026 leaderboard (ABB3 vs IgFold vs tFold-Ab vs Ibex vs Boltz-2 vs AF3) on one post-cutoff benchmark; I did not find one.
- Whether the FoldBench 45.4% vs 47.9% discrepancy is a typo, subset or version difference.
- OpenDDE and the "2026ARK-AB" benchmark are unverified preprint claims; "Chai-3" appears in one article title I saw but I could not verify it (no claims made).

## 2. Antibody/protein language models, nativeness and humanness scoring (incl. AbNatiV)

### Takeaway
Antibody language models (AntiBERTy, AbLang/AbLang-2, IgLM, BALM, IgBert/IgT5, p-IgGen, AbLingua and others) are trained on OAS-scale data, but much of what they learn is germline and mutational-process bias, not functional selection. This is the main critique (Olsen/Deane; Matsen group). AbNatiV (VQ-VAE, Sormanni lab) is a nativeness/humanness scorer, now extended to a paired model (AbNatiV2, mAbs 2026). On the authors' own benchmarks it matches or beats OASis, Humatch and p-IgGen on humanness classification and beats Humatch and ImmunoMatch on pairing. The link between humanness scores and clinical immunogenicity is weak or contested.

### Cited Findings

**Model landscape and training data**
- AntiBERTy: Transformer trained on 558M natural OAS sequences, a common baseline. IgLM: GPT-2-style generative model trained on the same 558M-sequence OAS data, conditioned on chain type and species, redesigns variable-length spans. AbLang: separate heavy and light models trained to restore missing residues. AntiBERTa: noted for paratope prediction. BALM: reported best in a comparison paper except binding F1 (86.2 vs 85.5 for AntiBERTy) — [search summary](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.13006); [Ab-RoBERTa/comparison listing](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.12112).
- IgBert / IgT5 (Kenlay et al., PLoS Comput Biol, Dec 2024): initialised from general protein models, pre-trained on >2 billion unpaired OAS sequences, fine-tuned on about 2 million paired sequences; reported best on sequence recovery, expression and binding-affinity prediction, with models and software released — [PLoS Comput Biol / PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11654935/); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.17889).
- Other antibody-specific models in 2024-2026: p-IgGen (paired generative, fine-tuned from unpaired pretraining; zero-shot benchmark claims) — [Bioinformatics](https://academic.oup.com/bioinformatics/article/40/11/btae659/7888884); FAbCon (causal LM on paired and unpaired) — [bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.05.22.594943v1.full); S2ALM (sequence-structure pre-training) — [Research](https://spj.science.org/doi/10.34133/research.0721); AbLingua (1.7B parameters, 1.4B unpaired OAS sequences, clustering to avoid train/test leakage; Commun Biol 2026) — [Commun Biol](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42003-026-10283-z).
- A 2026 paired-masking paper reports that training only on paired VH-VL sequences with preferential CDR masking is sufficient (pre-training on billions of unpaired sequences gave no measurable benefit) and up to 27% better binding-affinity prediction than benchmarked antibody models; this contradicts the unpaired-pretrain/paired-fine-tune recipe of IgBert/IgT5 and p-IgGen. Results are self-reported on different benchmarks — [npj-family paper (2026)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44488-026-00010-2).
- General vs antibody-specific: one paper found a large general protein LM more effective than an antibody-specific LM for structure prediction — [search listing](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.13006). AbBiBench (15 models, 14 binding-affinity datasets) found structure-conditioned inverse-folding models (ESM-IF highlighted) beat sequence-only language models — [AbBiBench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.04235). FLAb2 reportedly finds antibody-specific models better for binding and immunogenicity, and general protein models better for thermostability and expression — [AbBiBench-search listing citing FLAb2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.04235); [FLAb2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12767642/).

**Critical evaluations: germline bias and what LMs learn**
- Olsen, Moal, Deane (Bioinformatics 2024, btae618; bioRxiv 2024.02.02.578678): germline bias is built into antibody data, so a large part of every variable domain stays germline; they built AbLang-2 to predict non-germline residues and suggest diverse valid mutations. AntiBERTy and AbLang-1 predict a known valid amino acid with >90% accuracy but almost solely predict the germline. Model: github.com/oxpig/AbLang2 — [Bioinformatics](https://academic.oup.com/bioinformatics/article/40/11/btae618/7845256); [bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.02.02.578678.full.pdf).
- Matsen group, "Separating selection from mutation in antibody language models" (eLife): uses AbLang-2 as a case study; argues masked-LM predictions partly reflect nucleotide-level mutation processes (single-nucleotide-per-codon changes are far more likely) and that masked training memorises germline sequences. They propose DASMs that output selection factors directly — [eLife](https://elifesciences.org/articles/109644); [reviewed preprint](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/109644).
- FLAb2 (Chungyoun and Gray, bioRxiv Dec 2025): across 30 AI/biophysical models, most protein AI models show no significant correlation on 80% of developability datasets; germline edit distance accounts for about 40% of the apparent predictive power of protein LMs — [FLAb2 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12767642/).

**AbNatiV (Sormanni lab) and humanness metrics**
- AbNatiV v1 (Ramon, Ali, Atkinson ... Sormanni; Nat Mach Intell 6:74-91, Jan 2024; DOI 10.1038/s42256-023-00778-3): VQ-VAE trained with masked unsupervised learning on curated native repertoires of aligned Fv sequences; separate models for human VH, Vκ, Vλ and camelid VHH; gives an overall score plus a per-residue profile; includes an automated humanisation pipeline for nanobodies and VH/VL Fvs; AbNatiV-humanised nanobodies retained binding and stability at par or better than wild type. PyPI package is CC BY-NC-SA 4.0; commercial use requires a Cambridge Enterprise licence — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s42256-023-00778-3); [PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/abnativ); [Cambridge Enterprise](https://licensing.enterprise.cam.ac.uk/product/abnativ-method); [bioRxiv v1](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2023.04.28.538712.full.pdf).
- AbNatiV2 / p-AbNatiV2 (Ramon, Frassetto, Zhao, Xu, Greenig, Onuoha, Sormanni; mAbs 2026, DOI 10.1080/19420862.2026.2646361; bioRxiv 2025.10.31.685806): AbNatiV1 handled unpaired sequences only; AbNatiV2 models are each trained on >= 20 million sequences; p-AbNatiV2 gives residue- and sequence-level humanness for VH/VL pairs and learns pairing likelihood via noise-contrastive training. Code, models and a paired humanisation pipeline are on the group's GitLab — [mAbs](https://doi.org/10.1080/19420862.2026.2646361); [bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.10.31.685806.full.pdf).
- AbNatiV2 benchmark numbers (author-reported): p-AbNatiV2 picks the native VH/VL pair as more likely in 74.3% of held-out cases vs 60.1% for Humatch and 60.5% for ImmunoMatch; native pair in the top 5 of 50 scrambled pairs about 35% vs 18% (Humatch) and 16% (ImmunoMatch). Unpaired humanness PR-AUC: AbNatiV1 0.976 (highest mean), p-IgGen 0.952, Humatch 0.850; AbNatiV2 and the OASis 9-mer search fall within bootstrap error of AbNatiV1. On human VH with a more diverse test set, AbNatiV2 PR-AUC moves 0.965 to 0.937 and AbNatiV1 0.950 to 0.893 — [mAbs PDF](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/19420862.2026.2646361); [bioRxiv v1](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.10.31.685806v1.full.pdf).
- The AbNatiV2 paper frames sequence-level VHH-nativeness or humanness as a pre- and post-generation filter for diffusion, autoregressive or inverse-folding models, and residue-level profiles as a way to focus mutation proposals and developability edits — [mAbs](https://doi.org/10.1080/19420862.2026.2646361).
- Humatch (Chinery et al., bioRxiv 2024): fast, gene-specific joint humanisation. Reported on mixed-gene designs: 51% judged non-human by Humatch vs 28% by AbNatiV, 8% by Hu-mAb and 1% by OASis. My source does not say exactly what denominator this is (it reads as the fraction of mixed-gene designs each metric rejects, i.e. Humatch is strictest) — [Humatch preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.09.16.613210v1.full.pdf).
- BioPhi / OASis / Sapiens (Prihoda et al., mAbs 2022): OASis is a 9-mer-peptide-based humanness score against OAS; reported to separate human from non-human with high accuracy and to correlate with clinical immunogenicity; Sapiens humanisation was comparable to human experts on 177 antibodies. A BioPhi AUC of 0.966 vs 0.896 for T20 appears only in a secondary aggregator table and is unverified — [BioPhi preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.08.08.455394.full.pdf); [search listing](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8837241/table/t0002).
- Hu-mAb: a reviewer notes its training data is almost entirely murine so it humanises murine precursors well and supports only V-typed formats — [review (arXiv 2310.09991)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.09991). T20: older score based on the closest 20 human sequences (same sources).
- Generative humanisation (BigHat, Gordon et al.; ICLR 2024 workshop): treats humanisation as conditional sampling from an LM trained on human antibodies; reports wet-lab data that affinity-guided sampling outperforms unguided sampling and unguided samples ranked post hoc by an affinity oracle — [arXiv 2412.04737](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.04737).
- Humanness vs immunogenicity: a review of marketed antibody biotherapeutics found no statistically significant correlation between percent humanness and reported ADA rates; a 2020 PLoS One table likewise showed similar humanness scores for immunogenic and non-immunogenic antibodies; the 2013 BMC Biotechnology humanness-score paper reported that higher scores correlate with lower immunogenicity. Evidence conflicts and the surrogates are weak — [BMC Biotechnol 2013](https://www.biomedcentral.com/1472-6750/13/55); [PLoS One table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7458303/table/pone.0238150.t001); [patent-data humanness paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.14442).

### Inferences
- Distinguish three things that get conflated: germline-likeness (what masked LMs mostly capture), repertoire nativeness/humanness (what AbNatiV and OASis score), and functional fitness (binding, expression, developability). AbNatiV is deliberately about the second; the first is partly an artefact and the third is not captured by either (FLAb2 shows about 40% of apparent LM predictive power is germline distance).
- AbNatiV v1, AbNatiV2 and OASis are statistically indistinguishable on the authors' humanness PR-AUC, so the practical differentiators are residue-level interpretability, VHH support, pairing (p-AbNatiV2) and per-residue guidance, not raw AUC.
- Because humanness metrics are validated against "human vs non-human" origin, not clinical ADA, a high score is evidence of repertoire-likeness (and probably lower developability risk via naturalness), not of low immunogenicity.
- Independent head-to-head comparisons of humanness metrics outside the metric authors' own papers appear to be scarce (every comparison I found was authored by a metric developer).

### Gaps
- No sources retrieved for ESM-2, ESM-3, ESM-C, ProGen2, xTrimoPGLM, PALM-H3, AbMAP, BALM-paired, AbLang-2 benchmark numbers, or AntiBERTa details beyond one-line mentions; these need a separate pass.
- No head-to-head table of AbNatiV2 vs Hu-mAb/Sapiens/BioPhi on one paired humanisation task; AbNatiV2 supplementary tables are the likely place.
- The Humatch "51% / 28% / 8% / 1%" figure: denominator and definition unclear.
- No independent evaluation (not by metric authors) of AbNatiV on clinical immunogenicity; none found.
- Could not read AbNatiV2 limitations or experimental validation sections.

## 3. Developability prediction and datasets

### Takeaway
Developability is predictable only in limited ways. Surface hydrophobicity (HIC) is the one property with decent held-out performance (blinded Spearman about 0.71 in the Ginkgo 2025 competition); polyreactivity, self-association, titre and thermostability reach only about 0.3-0.4 Spearman, and most protein AI models do not beat simple baselines or germline distance. Rule-based and physics-informed tools (TAP, CamSol, DeepSP/SAP/SCM surrogates) remain the defensible baseline. The bottleneck is data: small (hundreds), heterogeneous across labs and assays, and mostly late-stage clinical antibodies. A pharma-Ginkgo-Apheris consortium (target 10,000 antibodies, announced 29 Sep 2026) is the field's response.

### Cited Findings

**Competitions and benchmarks**
- 2025 Ginkgo Datapoints Antibody Developability Competition (mAbs 2026; ran 8 Sep to 18 Nov 2025): public training set of 246 antibodies, blinded test set of 80; five properties (hydrophobicity, thermostability, self-association, titre, polyreactivity); 113 teams from 25 countries (38 companies, 39 universities). Best test Spearman rho: hydrophobicity 0.708, thermostability 0.392, polyreactivity 0.356, self-association 0.337, titre 0.310. Cross-validation scores consistently exceeded held-out scores (overfitting, poor out-of-distribution generalisation). Authors conclude datasets are too small and heterogeneous and call for large standardised data. A PubMed correction notice exists (contents not seen) — [mAbs](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/19420862.2026.2634216); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41724677/); [correction](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41819821/); [Oxford ORA record](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:f1c61639-22e2-4737-b651-f18888f6b507).
- FLAb2 (Chungyoun and Gray, bioRxiv 27 Dec 2025): >4M antibodies from 32 studies (Zenodo record cites 241 datasets), seven properties (thermostability, expression, aggregation, binding affinity, pharmacokinetics, polyreactivity, immunogenicity), 30 AI/biophysical models. Protein AI models show no significant correlation on 80% of datasets and no model works across all properties or across similar datasets; about 40% of apparent LM power is germline distance; fine-tuning with >= 100 points helps thermostability, aggregation and binding, but polyreactivity and pharmacokinetics lack enough data for significance; with about 1,000 points a fine-tuned one-hot model matches fine-tuned billion-parameter models. Training-data composition matters more than architecture; intrinsic biophysical properties (thermostability) are more learnable than extrinsic ones (immunogenicity, PK) — [FLAb2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12767642/); [bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2025.12.27.696706v1.full); [Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/records/21580361); [GitHub](https://github.com/Graylab/FLAb).
- Industry data: Amini et al. (Biogen, mAbs 2026) used 18,540 paired-chain antibodies from 33 historical programmes, targeting polyspecificity (PSR), HIC and AC-SINS; fine-tuning IgBert, IgT5 and ESM-2 on in-house data beats frozen embeddings; pre-trained perplexity gives a complementary unsupervised risk signal; adding PSR-sorted clones from synthetic human antibody libraries clearly improved PSR prediction, with smaller and less consistent gains for HIC and AC-SINS — [mAbs](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/19420862.2026.2647489); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13007433/).
- Non-specificity: Sakhnini et al. (Novo Nordisk, mAbs 2026) combine PLM embeddings with biophysical descriptors for polyreactivity/non-specificity and argue biophysical properties matter for this endpoint — [PMC13217913](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13217913/).
- High-throughput developability platform paper (bioRxiv 2025) frames ML for developability as being in its infancy — [bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.05.01.651684.full.pdf).
- Antibody Developability Consortium (Ginkgo Datapoints + Apheris; founding members AbbVie, argenx, Lundbeck, Takeda; announced 29 Sep 2026): aims for the largest standardised developability dataset, 10,000 antibodies, with federated training so raw proprietary sequences are not shared — [Business Wire](https://secure.businesswire.com/news/home/20260929556449/en/Ginkgo-Datapoints-and-Apheris-Welcome-AbbVie-argenx-Lundbeck-and-Takeda-as-Founding-Members-of-the-Antibody-Developability-Consortium); [Drug Target Review](https://www.drugtargetreview.com/ai-consortium-targets-antibody-developability-prediction-with-10000-sequence-dataset/2136627.article); [Apheris](https://www.apheris.com/networks/antibody-developability-consortium).

**Tools: TAP, CamSol, DeepSP/DeepSCM and others**
- TAP (Raybould et al., PNAS 2019): five guideline metrics from models of post-Phase I clinical-stage antibodies: total CDR length, patches of surface hydrophobicity, positive charge, negative charge in CDRs, and Fv charge asymmetry, with amber/red flags from percentile cut-offs. A 2024 Communications Biology update re-derives thresholds for ABodyBuilder1 and ABodyBuilder2 structures on a clinical-stage therapeutics set of about 661-664 entries (e.g. CDR length red flag > 63 in both; hydrophobicity red flag > 219.20 with ABB1, > 201.59 with ABB2). TAP is free on the Oxford OPIG server — [PNAS/PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6410772); [2024 update table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10774428/table/Tab1); [bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/359141.full.pdf).
- CamSol (Sormanni, Aprile, Vendruscolo, J Mol Biol 2015): sequence-based method for rational design of variants with enhanced solubility, screening tens of thousands of mutations; external testing on 56 variants of 19 wild-type proteins (some antibodies) vs SOLpro and PROSO II. Validated on full-length mAbs differing by up to 32 variable-domain residues, with predicted and measured solubility highly correlated; a library of 17 CamSol-designed humanised-mAb variants showed strong correlation between predicted and measured solubility and with in vitro non-specific interaction assays — [CamSol page (Vendruscolo group)](https://www-vendruscolo.ch.cam.ac.uk/camsolmethod.html); [Cambridge Enterprise](https://licensing.enterprise.cam.ac.uk/product/camsol-method); [mAb library screening](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5558012/); [humanised mAb variants](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6380425). The date of the full-length mAb paper (my summary says 2018) was not verified.
- DeepSP (Lai lab, Stevens; CSBJ 2024): CNN trained on 20,530 antibody sequences predicting 30 MD-derived spatial aggregation-propensity (SAP) and spatial charge map (SCM) properties from the Fv sequence; linear correlation with MD values 0.76-0.96 (mean 0.87); DeepSP descriptors used as features predicted aggregation rate for 21 antibodies with performance similar to MD-based approaches. DeepSCM is the earlier CNN surrogate for SCM aimed at high-concentration viscosity screening. Web app and GitHub "Lailabcode/AbDev" (a 12-property web app; not the same as the Ginkgo AbDev competition) — [DeepSP (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11140563/); [bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.02.28.582582.full.pdf). Note the surrogates reproduce MD descriptors, not experimental viscosity; the viscosity claim rests on SCM, not a direct viscosity benchmark in my sources.
- AbNatiV-based developability: AbNatiV2 proposes nativeness as a filter and per-residue profile for developability edits (no developability-assay validation seen in my sources) — [mAbs](https://doi.org/10.1080/19420862.2026.2646361).

### Inferences
- The held-out Spearman values from the Ginkgo competition (0.31-0.39 for four of five assays) are the best current calibration of how much to trust a developability reward model; guided generation built on such predictors inherits that noise.
- Because FLAb2 finds one-hot encodings match huge fine-tuned PLMs at about 10^3 points, and germline distance explains about 40% of PLM signal, gains will come from data scale and standardisation (consortium) more than from larger architectures.
- Physics/rule-based descriptors (TAP, CamSol, SAP/SCM surrogates) remain valuable as interpretable, assay-agnostic priors; the uncomfortable fact is that their head-to-head comparison against modern PLM predictors on blinded, standardised data is rare.
- Hydrophobicity (HIC/retention-time type readouts) is the "solved-ish" axis; polyreactivity, self-association/viscosity, titre and PK are not.

## 5. Open problems at the interface of nativeness/developability prediction and generative design

### Takeaway
Generative antibody design now has working hit rates (company-reported 16-20% for de novo designs on diverse targets, Germinal 4-22%), but developability is mostly handled by computational filters and in-silico reward models whose experimental accuracy is poor (Spearman about 0.3-0.4). Multi-objective approaches (constrained preference optimisation, Pareto-aligned diffusion, energy-based models, guided discrete diffusion, nativeness filters) exist but are validated mainly in silico. The open problems are reward reliability, trade-offs with affinity, calibration, and benchmark/data standards.

### Cited Findings
- De novo design hit rates (all company or preprint-reported): Chai-2 achieved about 16% hit rate with <= 20 designs per target across 52 antigens with no prior binders in the PDB and found binders for about 50% of targets (picomolar KD for some targets); no peer-reviewed developability data seen — [Chai-2 preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.07.05.663018.full.pdf); [Chai announcement](https://www.chaidiscovery.com/news/introducing-chai-2). Germinal: experimental success 4-22% per target with 43-101 designs tested per antigen; developability only as computational filters — [Germinal preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.09.19.677421v2.full). BoltzGen: 66% (6 of 9 targets with nM binders, <= 15 designs each), vendor documentation only — [Tamarind docs](https://docs.tamarind.bio/tools/boltzgen). RFantibody hit rates reported from 0.1% to 39% across studies, definitions vary — [agent-guided nanobody study](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.04.13.717816v1.full). No wet-lab developability data found for any of these tools.
- Multi-objective design (ICLR 2025 AbNovo): pre-trained structure-sequence diffusion model fine-tuned with constrained preference optimisation; objectives include binding energy and evolutionary plausibility, with constraints on non-specific binding and self-association — [ICLR 2025](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/101074481b94b857085a38994117511f-Paper-Conference.pdf).
- Pareto-Optimal Energy Alignment (Wen et al., NeurIPS 2025): multi-objective DPO-style alignment of an antibody diffusion model with online exploration to build a better Pareto front, targeting nature-like antibodies — [NeurIPS 2025](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/41f994cacb08c9d2efc9340117e454f0-Paper-Conference.pdf).
- Energy-based generative models for mAbs (mAbs 2025): explores trade-offs among solubility, humanness and affinity and generates candidates on Pareto fronts; in the model's predictions, raising the solubility score from 2 to 6 lowers affinity by about one order of magnitude (in-silico trade-off, not an experimental result) — [mAbs](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/19420862.2025.2584935); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.13390).
- Guided Generation for Developable Antibodies (Zhao, Moller, Quintero-Cadena, van Niekerk; ICML 2025; arXiv 2507.02670): discrete diffusion trained on paired OAS plus developability measurements of 246 clinical-stage antibodies, steered by Soft Value-based Decoding (SVDD); predicted developability scores are enriched vs unguided baselines (abstract-level; no wet-lab validation seen) — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.02670); [ICML](https://icml.cc/virtual/2025/51275).
- Generative co-design of CDR sequences and structures with black-box guidance in a shared latent space jointly optimising affinity and developability — [arXiv 2508.11424](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.11424).
- Generative humanisation (BigHat): conditional sampling from a human-antibody LM with affinity guidance, lab-validated against unguided and post-hoc ranked samples — [arXiv 2412.04737](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.04737).
- Genentech/Prescient Design lab-in-the-loop: multi-property optimisation with generative models, multi-task predictors and active learning; four targets (EGFR, IL-6, HER2, OSM) with improved affinity; walk-jump sampling with 92% expression rate and 12 model-update cycles reported in press and talk summaries (secondary) — [ICLR 2024 talk](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2024/invited-talk/21801); [STAT sponsor piece](https://www.statnews.com/sponsor/2024/06/07/making-drug-discovery-more-iterative-with-ai/); [PEGS 2026](https://www.pegsummit.com/pegs-ai/26/ml-ai-for-biologics-developability).
- Review: "Beyond affinity: AI-supported developability assessment and multi-objective optimization" (Antibody Therapeutics) covers Pareto optimisation, Bayesian optimisation, active learning and conditional generation, arguing models are most useful for prioritising experiments, not replacing them — [Antibody Therapeutics](https://academic.oup.com/abt/article/9/4/508/8748294).
- AbNatiV2 positions nativeness as a pre/post-generation filter and residue profiles as guidance for mutation proposals — [mAbs](https://doi.org/10.1080/19420862.2026.2646361).

### Inferences
Open problems at the interface (my synthesis; each follows from cited evidence):
1. **Reward reliability and reward hacking.** Guidance by developability predictors trained on 246 antibodies (best blinded rho 0.31-0.39 in the competition) is likely to exploit predictor errors, so guided samples will look good in silico and fail in the lab. Needed: uncertainty-aware, conservative guidance (ensembles, applicability-domain checks such as nativeness itself) and prospective wet-lab tests of guided vs unguided designs. Almost no such experiments exist in my sources apart from the BigHat humanisation study.
2. **Is nativeness a prior, a constraint or an objective?** Nativeness (AbNatiV) is an in-distribution density-like score; maximising it pushes sequences toward the repertoire mode and germline, where LMs are already biased. It may conflict with affinity-enhancing, non-germline mutations. A principled treatment (constraint above a threshold, or Pareto axis) and the shape of the nativeness-affinity trade-off are untested experimentally. Combining AbNatiV-like scores with selection-versus-mutation-corrected models (DASM-style) is an open direction.
3. **Does nativeness predict developability or immunogenicity?** No direct evidence in my sources; humanness-ADA is contested. Establishing this on standardised data (GDPa1-type, the new consortium) is a prerequisite for using nativeness as a developability proxy in design.
4. **Pareto optimisation with noisy, partly conflicting objectives.** Solubility-affinity trade-offs are shown in silico (energy-based models) and attacked with constrained preference optimisation (AbNovo) and Pareto-aligned diffusion, but there is no shared benchmark with experimental ground truth for more than two or three properties, and no calibration of how predicted Pareto fronts transfer to assays.
5. **Predictor-of-structure mismatch.** Developability descriptors (TAP, SAP/SCM, DeepSP) depend on predicted Fv structures whose H3 error is 2.4-2.9 Å; de novo designs are also validated with co-folding models whose Ab-Ag docking is wrong in over half of cases, so in-silico filters inherit structural error. Sensitivity of developability scores to H3 conformational ensembles is untested in my sources.
6. **Paired and format context.** Developability depends on VH/VL pairing and format; p-AbNatiV2 is a first step on pairing, but nanobody/VHH and bispecific/multi-specific formats lack comparable developability benchmarks.
7. **Evaluation standards for de novo design.** Hit-rate definitions differ widely (0.1% to 39% for the same family of tools), and developability of de novo binders is rarely measured; a blinded, standardised design-plus-developability benchmark (analogous to the Ginkgo competition, which only covered prediction) is missing.
